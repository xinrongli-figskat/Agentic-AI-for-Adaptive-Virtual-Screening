# Progress summary

## Data suitable for retrospective evaluation remain scarce

The project has screened ChEMBL 37 activity data and numerically evaluated 81 assay candidates. Sixty-two passed the initial numerical screen. Six have completed focused source review, and one currently supports a usable, source-limited evaluation set: the human TNAP assay. Its molecular structures and measured results can be reconstructed from the cited public sources, so this is a reproducible evaluation set rather than a blinded one. The outcome is not evidence that the other assays are absent; most remain unresolved because the source records do not yet establish compound identity or experimental meaning well enough.

This funnel—62 numerically promising assays, six source reviews, and one source-limited set—quantifies the shortage of suitable public assay data. It also shows why increasing the number of candidate molecules alone cannot solve the problem.

## Active-search comparisons

In the original comparison, using the strongest of our tested prediction methods and a seven-rule selection menu, five repeated language-model runs did not differ clearly from the best single fixed selection rule chosen after observing the results: the mean difference was −0.48 active compounds within 50 selections, with a 95% interval from −2.47 to 1.51. The interval includes zero. In **that original setting only**, the language model had higher point estimates than uniform policy mixing, a mixture matched to its observed rule frequencies, and the Thompson-sampling and UCB1 adaptive baselines. Those comparisons are exploratory and do not establish a general advantage: the language model’s action choices vary between runs, and the adaptive-baseline comparisons do not share the same repeat design.

A separate TNAP comparison used a moderately predictive method (cross-validated ROC-AUC 0.821) and a five-rule menu. Here the two fixed scored rules differed substantially: pure ranking found 16.65 active compounds within 50 selections, whereas diversity-penalised ranking found 9.65. The language-model controller found 11.33, or 5.33 fewer than pure ranking (95% interval −7.83 to −2.93). Its differences from Thompson sampling, UCB1, and an equally weighted rule mixture had intervals that included zero. About 65% of its executed decisions used diversity-penalised ranking. The favourable point-estimate comparison with those adaptive baselines in the original setting therefore did **not** carry over to this setting.

To separate a stable rule preference from useful timing, we ran deterministic CPU controls that drew rules at the language model’s observed frequencies, independently of the feedback at each decision. In the five-rule setting, this control found 11.20 actives on average, versus 11.33 for the language model: the paired difference was +0.13 (95% interval −0.65 to 0.98). We could not detect a timing contribution beyond its overall rule preference. In the original prediction setting, a coarsened three-behaviour comparison gave 24.90 for the frequency control and 24.80 for the language model, a difference of −0.10 (95% interval −2.25 to 1.80). That second comparison is descriptive: the original seven-rule menu included lookahead and logistic-regression choices that cannot be made identical to the five-rule control by relabelling. Neither comparison supports a claim that the language model timed its policy changes better than a frequency-matched control.

We then let the controller see the full preset TNAP library, including molecular structures and current prediction scores, while keeping all unselected experimental outcomes hidden. With the same five-rule menu and initial compounds, it found 14.25 actives within 50 selections across 20 seeds, compared with 11.33 for the earlier controller that saw only numerical summaries. The paired gain was 2.93 actives (seed-bootstrap 95% interval 0.42 to 5.55; 0.19 to 5.80 after adding the measured between-run model variation). Diversity-penalised selection fell from 65% to 21% of executed batches. The broader view therefore changed the controller’s choices and improved its result in this setting, but it remained below the fixed pure-ranking rule by 2.40 actives (95% interval −4.30 to −0.80). This is one retrospective dataset and one run per seed; the system instruction was also adapted to describe the visible library, so the comparison should not be read as an isolated causal effect of molecular structures.

Reproducibility is a material limitation. When the request settings and prompt inputs were byte-for-byte identical, the model selected different rules in 38.5% of 200 paired requests. Across repeated runs with the same random seed, the standard deviation in the number of actives found within 50 selections was 2.19. One malformed structured response was observed in the initial request series, approximately 1% of that segment; the response was retained and handled under a bounded retry rule. A single run is therefore one sample from a variable process, and comparisons require repeated runs.

## Prediction methods and policy switching

Six prediction methods had cross-validated ROC-AUC values from 0.771 to 0.897 on TNAP. Greater model complexity did not imply greater predictive strength. With the same Morgan molecular fingerprints, nearest-neighbour similarity ranking reached 0.897, logistic regression 0.844, and gradient boosting 0.803. Across the six methods, the difference between the best and worst fixed selection rules showed no systematic monotonic relation to prediction AUC.

A further pattern concerned switching between rules. In four of six prediction-method settings, the best adaptive controller did worse than every fixed rule in that setting; in one it tied the fixed rules, and in one it did better than some fixed rules. Thus, in those four settings, the loss was not only from choosing a suboptimal rule: switching among rules itself failed to recover the performance of any fixed choice. The language-model controller came close to the best fixed rule only in the original seven-rule comparison; the separate five-rule comparison above shows why that observation should not be generalised.

## Measurement effort and remaining limitations

“Measurement effort” here means the number of separate concentration–response determinations recorded for a compound in the source deposition. It does not mean the number of members in a chemical series. On TNAP, the median was one and the maximum was 21. This measure predicted the measured active/inactive class with ROC-AUC 0.879, compared with 0.598 for the strongest individual structural descriptor. The source’s dose-response records indicate selective follow-up of compounds that appeared promising. The association is therefore a data-collection bias, not a usable chemical feature. The available record does not establish that series membership alone caused the association.

The project remains a pedagogical prototype using a small number of retrospective public assay sets. TNAP source identity is only partially resolved, DYRK3 is small and has a high active fraction, and the language model’s current role is mainly to choose among predefined rules. Runtime checks in the library-visible experiment confirmed that the preset library was present while unselected measured outcomes remained hidden. The observed improvement does not establish a general agentic advantage over a strong fixed rule, and it does not test whether the benefit changes systematically with prediction-method strength. A negative result for agentic active search at month end remains an acceptable conclusion if the broader evidence warrants it.
